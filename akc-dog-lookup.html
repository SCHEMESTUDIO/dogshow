You've got a registration number scribbled on a piece of paper, or a breeder who swears the litter is "fully AKC," and you want to know what that actually means before you hand over a deposit. Why Look Up AKC Records?

An AKC registration links a dog to a documented line of ancestry. Once a determination of eligibility is met, either by litter application or registry research, the dog can be registered as purebred by the AKC, though registration indicates only that the dog's parents were registered as one recognized breed. That's the whole point of checking a number: it confirms lineage on paper, nothing more and nothing less.

People usually look up a record for one of three reasons: they've lost their own dog's paperwork, they want to check that a breeder's claims match what's actually on file, or they're researching a litter before they commit to a puppy. AKC registration paperwork is a good way to verify a dog's pedigree, but some sellers forge it to fetch a higher price, or promise papers later and never follow through. A five-minute lookup won't catch every scam, but it closes the easiest one.

How to Use the AKC Lookup Tool

The AKC's dog search sits inside its Reports & Pedigree section on akc.org. It lets you search for dogs and track their points progression, and logging in gives you access to a 'Dog Search' tab with additional details. You don't need an account to run a basic search, but AKC has folded more of its record access behind a free login over the past few years, so creating one saves you a step if you plan to look up more than one dog.

Finding a dog by registration number

This is the cleanest path. Not having the registration number can make finding a specific dog more difficult, since the number provides the most accurate and efficient lookup results. Type it in exactly as it appears on the paperwork, no spaces added or removed, and the tool should return the dog's registered name, breed, and sex, plus a pedigree option.

Searching without a registration number

No number? You can still search. If you know the registered dog's name but not its AKC registration number, you can search by name, and vice versa. The catch is that common names produce a pile of results, especially in popular breeds, so the more precise the spelling (including any kennel prefix), the faster you'll narrow it down.

If you're trying to confirm a puppy's parents rather than the puppy itself, ask the breeder for the sire and dam's registered names or numbers and run those instead. A legitimate breeder should hand this over without hesitation.

What the record tells you (and doesn't)

A returned record typically shows the registered name, breed, sex, and a multi-generation pedigree. It includes a listing of the selected dog's ancestry for four or five generations. What it does not include is any owner contact information. Due to privacy policies, personal contact details aren't displayed publicly through basic searches. So a lookup confirms that a dog exists in the registry with a given lineage, useful for verifying paperwork, but not for tracking down a current owner or a lost dog. That's what microchip registries are for.

Common Lookup Scenarios

Lost your dog's registration paperwork. If you registered the dog yourself, logging into your AKC account should surface it under your dog's profile. If you're not the original registrant, or the litter was registered by the breeder, you may need to contact AKC customer service directly with whatever details you have (name, breed, approximate whelping date) to track down the number.

Checking a breeder's claims. Run the sire and dam's numbers too, since a litter application is what actually creates the eligibility. AKC registration primarily verifies the lineage of a dog and confirms that the parents are also registered with the AKC. If a breeder can't or won't produce numbers for the parents, treat that as a bigger red flag than a slow website.

Researching a litter before you buy. Beyond the basic lookup, AKC's Marketplace lists breeders and can show whether a breeder participates in AKC programs, letting you search by breed, location, and whether they take part in programs like Bred with H.E.A.R.T. That program flag is a decent secondary signal, though it's not a substitute for visiting the breeder or asking about health testing directly.

What an AKC Record Doesn't Tell You

This is the part worth sitting with before you get too comfortable with a clean lookup result. Registration indicates only that the dog's parents were registered as one recognized breed; it does not necessarily indicate that the dog comes from healthy or show-quality bloodlines, nor is registration necessarily a reflection on the quality of the breeder or how the puppy was raised.

A registration number confirms paperwork, not welfare. It won't tell you whether the parents were hip- or eye-tested, whether the puppies were raised underfoot in a home or in a barn out back, or whether the breeder has ever had a health complaint filed against them. It's worth remembering that it's the litter that gets registered, not the breeder, and the AKC does not recommend or endorse breeders. A forged pedigree is rare, but a legitimate pedigree attached to a careless breeding program is not.

If you want that fuller picture, ask directly for OFA hip and elbow clearances, eye certifications relevant to the breed, and, ideally, a chance to meet at least one parent. None of that shows up in a registration search, and no honest breeder will treat the question as an insult.

Beyond AKC: Other Resources for Breed and Breeder Info

AKC registration is one data point among several. Pet recovery and microchip registries are a separate system entirely, worth checking if the concern is a lost dog rather than a pedigree. Registries participating in the AAHA Universal Pet Microchip Lookup Tool, such as AKC Reunite, HomeAgain, and Pawbase, exist specifically to reunite lost pets with owners. Breed-specific parent clubs are another good stop. They often keep their own health-testing recommendations and sometimes maintain breeder referral lists that go further than AKC's baseline requirements.
