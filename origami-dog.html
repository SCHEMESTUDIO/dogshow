Origami Dog: Easy Fold Guide + Free Printable

Fold a classic origami dog face in eight simple steps using one square sheet of paper — no glue, no scissors, about fifteen minutes start to finish.

Quick answer: start with a square, colored side down, crease the diagonal, fold the ears down at an angle (leaving a small gap at the top), fold the crown and chin, flip it over, and draw on a face. That's the whole classic dog-face fold.

Materials

What you'll need

You don't need scissors, glue, or a craft cabinet. One square sheet of paper does the whole job.

Kami sheets come pre-cut in squares, commonly 15×15 cm or 24×24 cm, colored on one side with white on the other side. That size range is the sweet spot for a dog face — big enough to fold comfortably, small enough that the finished piece doesn't flop.

If you don't have origami paper on hand, for a beginner trying one or two simple models, copy paper is fine, though anyone who wants to fold regularly or attempt anything beyond 10 steps will find proper origami paper worth the cost. The catch with printer paper is memory: origami paper holds a crease and stays folded, while printer paper has more "spring" and wants to unfold slightly, making sharp corners and thin points harder to achieve. It'll still make a perfectly good dog face — the ears just won't sit quite as crisply.

One more practical note: origami starts from a perfect square, while copy paper is rectangular, so you'll need to trim it into a square before you start. Fold one corner over to make a triangle, cut off the leftover strip, and you're square.

Step by step

How to fold an easy origami dog face

This is the classic dog-face fold — the one nearly every origami book teaches first, because it only asks for valley folds (no reverse folds, no complicated pockets).

1
Colored side down
Start with your square, colored side down. If using kami paper, this puts the white side facing up.
2
Crease the diagonal
Fold in half diagonally, corner to corner, to form a triangle. Crease firmly, then unfold — you'll use this as a guide line.
3
Refold the diagonal
Refold along that same diagonal, leaving it folded this time. You now have a triangle with the point facing you.
4
Fold down the ears
Fold the left and right corners down so they meet the bottom point, forming two floppy triangular "ears."
5
Angle the ears (the tricky one)
Instead of folding flush, angle the ears slightly outward and leave a small gap at the top — this is what gives real dimension instead of a flat triangle.
6
Fold the crown
Fold the top point down to create the crown of the head, tucking it just behind the top edges of the ears.
7
Fold the chin
Fold the bottom point up slightly to form the chin and give the face some depth.
8
Add the face
Flip the whole model over. Draw on eyes, a nose, and a mouth — marker, pen, or a small cut-out circle all work.

Common folding mistakes to avoid

Most origami dog faces go wrong in one of three places. First, skipping the crease-and-unfold in step 2 — that guide line is what keeps the ears symmetrical later, and eyeballing it usually leaves one ear bigger than the other. Second, folding the ears flat instead of leaving that small gap in step 5, which is the single biggest difference between a flat triangle and a dog that actually looks like it has ears. Third, using paper that's too thick — anything heavier than standard printer weight tends to crack rather than crease cleanly at the nose point.

Go further

Origami dog variations

Once the face fold feels easy, these two variations use the same basic diagonal-and-pleat logic but take the dog further.

Sitting Dog Fold

This version builds a body beneath the head instead of stopping at the face. After folding the sides to the center line and turning the paper over, you fold the top point down to the bottom corner, then fold that flap upward to create a pleat — repeating the pleat fold on the folded color portion gives the model its layered, seated posture. The pleats stack to form what reads as haunches, so the dog appears to be sitting rather than lying flat.

Full-Body Puppy Fold

For a dog with a visible tail and legs, the process extends further: after the head and body pleats are set, you fold the bottom corner to the bottom of the folded color portion and fold that flap down to create another pleat, then fold the top corner down to the bottom of the top pleat to shape the back half of the body. A final mountain fold down the middle, followed by a quarter turn, brings the head down using the creases made in the previous steps — that last move is what pops the whole thing into a standing, four-legged shape instead of a flat folded strip.

For families

Tips for folding with kids

Origami dog faces are a genuinely good rainy-afternoon activity, but a few adjustments make it easier for smaller hands. Start with the larger paper size rather than the small 15 cm squares — more surface area means more room for error before a fold looks obviously wrong. Let kids skip the "unfold and recrease" guide step if they're getting frustrated; a slightly asymmetrical dog with personality beats a perfect one nobody finished. And save the marker-drawn face for last, after the folding is done, so ink doesn't smear across wet fingers mid-fold.
